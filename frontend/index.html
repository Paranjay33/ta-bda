Why Hinglish is hard to read with ordinary sentiment tools

The same word, written five different ways.

accha acha achha

Three spellings of the same word — "good" — all carrying the same sentiment. A model trained only on English can't tell they match.

"movie acchi thi BUT ending bakwas thi"

One sentence, two sentiments in two languages — positive about the movie, negative about the ending. Hinglish sentiment means resolving both halves, not averaging them away.

Research insight · E4 − E3 on the locked test split

Loading verified results…

Reported midterm results — not reproducible, not locked-test

The midterm reported E3 74.10% and E4 85.16% Macro-F1 (+11.06 pp). The audit found the E3 figure was a validation score, that 1,446 of the 3,000 supplied validation rows were copies of training rows, and that no E4 checkpoint was saved. Both models were therefore retrained on a verified, de-duplicated split; the number above comes only from that re-run.

About this project

An academic project in two parts

Text Analytics

Understanding Hinglish sentence by sentence — cleaning noise while preserving sentiment-bearing signal, comparing a general multilingual model against one built specifically for code-mixed text, and evaluating how well each tells Positive, Negative, and Neutral apart.

Big Data Analytics

Processing the dataset with Apache Spark — DataFrame-based pipelines, Spark ML for the classical experiments, and a look at how the pipeline scales, run locally rather than on a multi-node cluster.

HinglishSense is a research prototype trained on SemEval-2020 Task 9 (SentiMix) tweets. It estimates the overall sentiment of a sentence; it does not reliably understand sarcasm or context, and its scores are not a measure of anyone's real feelings.
